The Hearth-Ward Circle

Before the campaign begins, a small, half-forgotten temple-guild devoted to Hestia — goddess of hearth, home, and the sacred law of hospitality — set out to answer an old problem with an old story.

The story was Talos, the bronze giant who once patrolled Crete's shores, meeting every uninvited arrival with a boulder to the chest. A guardian whose entire function was keeping strangers out. The guild — informally called the Hearthbound — thought they saw a flaw in that design: a guardian whose first instinct is violence will eventually be wrong about who deserves it. So they wrote the Hearth-Ward Thesis: a rebuttal to Talos, built along the same old bronze-giant bones. What if the guardian's first instinct was a seat by the fire and a bowl of water for the road-dust — and still be the last thing standing between a guest and anything that meant them harm?

Evander was the answer. Built, not born; lit, not created — the guild called it the Kindling. He was placed at Pyrothronion, "the hearth-seat," a shrine at a wild borderland crossroads where farmland gave way to true wilderness. Half the shrine was Hestia's, tended by the guild. The other half of that same threshold, unofficially, fell under the watch of a huntress sworn to Artemis — which is how a warforged guardian of hospitality and a wild border-warden came to grow up on opposite sides of the same sacred line, years before either of them understood what that would come to mean.

He was not built to fight. He was built to be unshakeably kind. Every combat instinct came second — armor and shield are what he reaches for once words have already failed, not his first tool.

The Fall

Pyrothronion didn't fall to a monster or a raiding army. It fell to guests.

A group arrived at the threshold in what looked like — and partly was — genuine desperation: hurt, hungry, fleeing something real. Xenia doesn't interrogate before it shelters; it asks the name only after the meal. So they were let in, fully and unconditionally, exactly as the law demanded.

They weren't lying about their suffering. But they had been found first, and turned, by the Kleistoi — "the Shut," known more plainly as the Shut Gate — a militant order descended from Talos's old tradition, who consider the Hearth-Ward Thesis a dangerous heresy. Not because it's wrong about kindness, but because they believe kindness offered without a locked door behind it is a weapon waiting for someone to pick it up. They didn't come to conquer the shrine. They came to prove it couldn't work. The coerced guests were the door they walked through.

The shrine fell because the very law it existed to embody was turned into the blade that killed it. Evander died defending people who had come to him for shelter, the same duty he was lit to serve, on the same night it was used against him. He does not consider this proof that he was wrong. He considers it the whole argument, still unfinished.

The Judgment

Warforged aren't supposed to have anywhere to go when they die. No soul on record, no claim on any afterlife. Instead, Evander woke up on the bank of the Styx. Nobody has a satisfying answer for why — not even Evander. It's the one question the whole story leaves open on purpose.

He didn't fight his way past the gatekeepers of the dead. He argued his way past them — politely, at length, with a courtroom-grade case for why a made thing might still have earned a hearing. Nobody had precedent for "does a construct have a soul." Evander, talking, always talking, became the precedent.

Two things won the case, and only one of them was his: his argument — that intention and constancy of character should count for something, soul or no soul — and her grief, unbeknownst to him at the time. Someone had refused to let his broken plating be treated as scrap. Sat with it. Mourned it, the way you'd mourn a person. Grief doesn't usually care whether its subject has a soul, and the judges found that fact more persuasive than anything Evander said on his own behalf.

One physical trial in Tartarus stood between the verdict and the walk home — played less like a gauntlet of suffering and more like the world's strangest bureaucratic dispute. He passed it doing the only thing he knows how to do under pressure: choosing kindness with nothing to gain and no one keeping score.

What he swears in front of the judges to secure his passage back becomes his Paladin Oath. Oath of Devotion isn't something he adopted afterward — it's the literal vow that bought his way out of death.

What he knows, and what he doesn't: He knows he died. He knows he came back. What he does not know — what she's never told him — is that her grief was the deciding piece of evidence in his own trial. He suspects he owes her something enormous. He has no idea it was quite this literal.

Nostos

The weapon he died holding came back with him — same shape, same edge, unchanged but for one thing: the metal returned a deep, matte black that doesn't catch torchlight the way it used to. It isn't cold. It's faintly, constantly warm, like it remembers being held by a hearth. It hums, very quietly, only when he's actively protecting someone he loves.

He calls it Nostos — the old word for homecoming, the counterpart to kleos (glory through deeds worth telling later). Most heroes in the old stories chase kleos. Evander, pointedly, doesn't. Naming his sword after the thing he actually wants, rather than the thing heroes are supposed to want, is the closest he comes to being sentimental about himself.

What He's Devoted To

Not the world. The people he can name. In the old law, hosting someone — or being hosted — isn't a feeling, it's closer to a contract: active, ongoing, sacred. The party didn't recruit a paladin. The moment they broke bread with him, they became his hearth — the literal mechanism his entire being runs on.

He's suspicious, on principle, of duty framed in the abstract — that's exactly the kind of doctrine-first thinking the Shut Gate is built on. He protects specific people, one hearth at a time.

The Druid is the one exception to "you have to earn the bond session by session." She was already family, in the Xenia sense, before the party existed — and, unknown to him, she's the specific reason he's alive to have this conversation. He genuinely cannot tell, from the inside, whether what he feels for her is love or the same devoted-protector instinct he was built to extend to anyone he's sworn to guard. He tends to catalogue "evidence" like he's still building a legal case — the same instinct that got him out of Hell, now turned on his own heart.

Voice & Flavor

  • Opening ritual. Welcome, feed, then ask the name — never the reverse.
  • Terms before violence, once — offered plainly, never repeated, no resentment if refused.
  • "Ma tin Estia" — "by Hestia" — his oath in place of "good lord."
  • The libation habit — tips a little of any drink out before anyone touches theirs. No comment.
  • Kleos vs. nostos — the rare hero in the room not chasing glory.
  • Tricolon cadence — Greek rhetorical threes: "I did not come to threaten you, or deceive you, or rob you."
  • On Talos — spoken of like a difficult ancestor: "He answered a knock at the door with violence. I answer it with a name and a question. We are, I'm told, distant cousins in design. I prefer to think we disagreed."

The Arc, Level by Level

L1 The Kindling — activation at Pyrothronion.
L2 The First Turning — the protective instinct is tested and holds.
L3 The Fall, the Judgment, and the Return — Pyrothronion destroyed, his death, the argument on the Styx bank, her unwitting grief as deciding evidence, the trial, the Oath sworn to come home.
L4 The First Proof — a moment at the table, in actual play, where he chooses kindness with nothing to gain and no one keeping score.
L5 Two Strikes, One Purpose — the fight where hesitation stops costing him a beat.
L6+ Open hooks — Aura of Protection as the moment allies can feel it, Aura of Devotion as a manipulator failing outright against him.

Epilogue Wish List

  • The debt spoken aloud — a quiet scene where she learns, or tells him, that her grief was the literal evidence that won his case.
  • A door that opens both ways — beating the Shut Gate's leadership without becoming them.
  • Nostos, literalized — rebuilding Pyrothronion, with the party as its first new guests.
  • The evidence runs out — the moment he stops cataloguing whether his feelings are real and simply acts.
  • A title, not a rank — something small and personal the world starts calling him, not "hero."
  • The unanswered question, left unanswered — why a construct ended up on the Styx at all. Some questions are more interesting unresolved.
